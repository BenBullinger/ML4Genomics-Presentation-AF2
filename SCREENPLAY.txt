[SLIDE 1. The problem: sequence to structure. Evita. Ends at 1:39.]

EVITA

(Point at the sequence on the left.)

A protein is a chain of amino acids. In the chain we call each amino acid a residue. There are
twenty kinds. Every residue has the same backbone atoms, a nitrogen, a carbon called C alpha, a
second carbon and an oxygen. The side chain hangs off the C alpha and differs from kind to kind. In
the cell the chain folds into one three dimensional shape, and that shape decides what the protein
does.

(Point at the sentence with the two numbers.)

Sequencing is cheap, so billions of sequences are known. A structure is measured by X-ray
crystallography or electron microscopy, which takes months or years. When the paper was written, the
Protein Data Bank, the database of measured structures, held about one hundred thousand distinct
proteins.

(Point at the picture.)

The task is to predict the position of every heavy atom, that is every atom except hydrogen, from
the sequence alone. The picture shows one protein. Green is the measured structure, blue is
AlphaFold's prediction.

[SLIDE 3. Confidence: pLDDT against measured lDDT. Evita. Ends at 3:46.]

EVITA

AlphaFold also says how much it trusts each residue. The local distance difference test, lDDT,
compares a predicted structure with the measured one. It takes pairs of atoms that are close to each
other in the measured structure and checks whether their distance is the same in the prediction. It
is local because it looks only at nearby pairs, and it needs no overlay of the two structures. The
score runs from 0 to 100.

pLDDT is the predicted lDDT. AlphaFold outputs it for every residue. It is trained on the network's
own mistakes. During training the predicted structure is scored against the truth, and a small
output layer learns to predict that score.

(Point at the plot.)

The plot shows 10,795 protein chains. The horizontal axis is the mean pLDDT of a chain, the vertical
axis the measured lDDT. The fitted line has slope 0.997 and an offset of about one point, so when
AlphaFold says 90, the measured score is about 89. The correlation is 0.76.


[SLIDE 4. The network: inputs, Evoformer, structure module. Evita. Ends at 4:57.]

EVITA

(Point from left to right while speaking.)

This is the whole network, Figure 1e of the paper. The input is one sequence.

(Point at the MSA box.)

The sequence is searched against genetic databases. The hits are sequences of related proteins. They
are written row by row and aligned column by column into a multiple sequence alignment, the MSA.

(Point at the templates box.)

The sequence is also searched against the structure database. Known structures of related proteins
become templates.

(Point at the two arrays.)

From the MSA and the templates the network builds two arrays. The MSA representation has one row per
sequence and one column per residue. The pair representation has one entry per pair of residues.

(Point at the Evoformer, then at the structure module.)

The Evoformer is a stack of 48 blocks that updates the two arrays. The structure module has 8 blocks
and turns the arrays into coordinates, coloured by confidence, blue is high and orange is low.

(Point at the recycling bar.)

Then the whole network runs again three times, with its own outputs as extra inputs. The paper calls
this recycling. The network outputs the 3D coordinates directly, and it is trained end to end.


[SLIDE 5. The design: four constraints built into the network. Evita. Ends at 6:16.]

EVITA

Before we look inside the blocks, here is the plan of the talk. The paper says the network is based
on the evolutionary, physical and geometric constraints of protein structures. We picked four such
constraints. Each is a fact about proteins that is known before training, and each is built into one
part of the network.

(Point row by row.)

One. Two residues that touch in the folded protein change together across homologous proteins, that
is related proteins. So the network attends over their alignment, the MSA.

Two. All pairwise distances must fit one three dimensional structure. Three residues form a
triangle, and the three distances of a triangle constrain each other. So the pair features are
updated in triangles of three residues.

Three. A protein that is rotated or moved in space is the same protein. So the network gives every
residue its own coordinate system and attends over distances, which do not change under rotation.

Four. A protein and its mirror image are different molecules. Chemists call this chirality. So the
loss compares atom positions residue by residue, in a way that tells the two apart.


[SLIDE 6. The input: the multiple sequence alignment. Evita. Ends at 7:21.]

EVITA

(Point at the rows and the columns.)

Constraint one in detail. This is a small alignment. The first row is the input sequence, every
other row is a homologous protein, a relative of it. Each column is one aligned position.

(Point at the two grey columns.)

Look at the two grey columns. In the first row the left column has K, lysine, which is positively
charged, and the right column has E, glutamate, which is negatively charged. In the lower rows it is
the other way round, E on the left and K or R on the right, R is arginine and also positive. The two
positions swap their charges together, so the two residues keep attracting each other. That happens
when the two positions touch in the folded protein. Such a pair is called a contact.

Marks and colleagues computed statistics of such co-variation in 2011 and predicted contacts from
them. AlphaFold takes the alignment itself as input, and the attention layers extract the patterns.


[SLIDE 7. The representations: MSA array and pair array. Evita. Ends at 8:03.]

EVITA

(Point left, then right.)

The network stores the alignment and the pairs as two arrays of vectors. The MSA representation has
one vector per cell of the alignment, with 256 numbers. The pair representation has one vector per
ordered pair of residues, with 128 numbers. Row i, column j describes how residue i relates to
residue j.

At the start the pair array is built from the sequence, the distance of i and j along the chain, the
templates, and a first pass over the alignment. Everything else is learned.

Every Evoformer block reads these two arrays and writes them back.


[SLIDE 8. Evoformer: one block. Evita. Ends at 9:23.]

EVITA

This is one Evoformer block, Figure 3a. The MSA array runs along the top, the pair array along the
bottom.

(Point along the top row.)

In the top row, each sequence first attends along its own row, over the residues. Then each column
attends along itself, over the sequences. A transition layer follows, a small two layer network
applied to every cell.

(Point at the red arrow into the outer product mean, and at the arrow out of it.)

Now each array updates the other. MSA to pair goes through the outer product mean. For every pair of
columns i and j, the network takes the vector of column i and the vector of column j in each
sequence, forms their outer product, and averages over all sequences. The result updates the pair i
j. This is where the co-variation we saw on the alignment becomes a pair feature.

(Point at the pair to MSA arrow.)

Pair to MSA is on the next slide.

(Point at the bracket.)

Then the pair array gets four triangle layers and a transition.

The paper describes the pair array as a graph. Every residue is a node, every pair of residues is an
edge, and the network infers which pairs are in contact. There are 48 such blocks and none of them
share weights.


[SLIDE 9. Evoformer: row attention with pair bias. Evita. Ends at 10:29.]

EVITA

Here is the row attention in detail, Supplementary Figure 2.

(Point at the MSA input, then at the queries and keys.)

We are inside one sequence s. Residue i has a query vector, residue j has a key vector. Their dot
product says how much i should attend to j.

(Point at the blue box and at the plus.)

The pair array adds a bias. A linear layer maps the pair vector z i j to one number b i j per
attention head, and that number is added to the dot product before the softmax. So the attention
weight of i on j is the softmax over j of q transposed k, divided by the square root of the vector
length c, plus b i j.

(Point at the gate.)

On the right, a sigmoid gate computed from the same input multiplies the output.

So the pair representation biases the attention within every sequence. And the sequences, through
the outer product mean, update the pairs. The paper says that this closes the loop between the two
arrays.


[SLIDE 10. Evoformer: triangle updates. Evita. Ends at 12:05.]

EVITA

Constraint two, the triangles. A set of pairwise distances is not automatically a shape. For three
residues i, j and k, the distance from i to j can be no larger than the distance i k plus the
distance k j. The paper does not enforce this. It arranges the pair updates so that the edge i j is
always updated from the two edges that complete a triangle with it, i k and j k, for every third
residue k.

(Point at the two graphs on the left.)

There are two kinds. The multiplicative update takes a projection of edge i k and a projection of
edge j k, multiplies them element by element, and sums over k. The formula is on the slide. Edges
have a direction, from the first residue to the second. The outgoing version uses the two edges that
leave i and j towards k, the incoming version the two edges that arrive at i and j from k.

(Point at the two graphs on the right.)

The triangle attention lets the edge i j attend to the edges i k that start at the same node, with
the third edge j k as a bias. The second version does the same around the ending node j.

Each block runs all four. The paper reports that either kind alone already gives accurate
structures, and both together are more accurate.

[SLIDE 11. Structure module: residues as rigid bodies. Ben. Ends at 13:38.]

BEN

Thank you, Evita. The structure module, Figure 3d, turns the two arrays into atoms. This is
constraint three. The module takes the pair representation and the single representation, which is a
projection of the first row of the MSA array, the input sequence.

(Point at the frames at the bottom left.)

The key object is the frame. A frame is a rotation and a translation, one per residue, written T i.
It is a small coordinate system placed on the backbone atoms N, C alpha and C of that residue. At
the start all frames sit at the origin with the same orientation.

(Point at the IPA box, then at the two purple boxes.)

Each of the 8 layers does two things, and the layers share their weights. Invariant point attention,
IPA, updates the single representation. I open it on the next slide. A linear layer then predicts a
small rotation and translation per residue, which is composed onto its frame, so every frame moves.

(Point at the green box.)

The network also predicts torsion angles, the rotations around the backbone and side chain bonds.
After the last layer the atoms are placed from the frames and these angles, with ideal bond lengths
and angles.

(Point at the inset.)

The residues move as free rigid bodies. The bond geometry between residues is repaired afterwards,
by an extra loss term late in training and by a short physics based optimisation of the final atom
positions.


[SLIDE 12. Structure module: invariant point attention (IPA). Ben. Ends at 15:06.]

BEN

Invariant point attention, Supplementary Figure 8. It is attention over residues, with three terms
in the score.

(Point at the red box.)

The first is the usual dot product between the query of residue i and the key of residue j, both
from the single representation.

(Point at the blue box.)

The second is the pair bias b i j from the pair array.

(Point at the green box.)

The third is geometric. Every residue also produces query points and key points, three dimensional
points in its own frame. The network maps the query points of i and the key points of j into the
common global frame, with the frames T i and T j, and subtracts the squared distance between them,
weighted by a learned factor gamma.

(Point at the formula.)

The three terms are added and passed through a softmax over j.

(Point at the invert box.)

The values also come with points. They are summed in the global frame and mapped back into the frame
of residue i, in the invert box.

Why invariant. If we rotate or move the whole protein, every frame moves with it. The distances
between the placed points do not change, and the points mapped back into frame i do not change. So
the attention weights are invariant to a global rotation and translation. The algebra is on backup
slide one.


[SLIDE 13. The loss: frame aligned point error (FAPE). Ben. Ends at 16:44.]

BEN

Constraint four, the loss. It is called frame aligned point error, FAPE, Figure 3f.

(Point at the figure.)

Green is the predicted chain, grey the true one. Take a frame k on the predicted chain, and the same
frame k on the true chain. Take an atom j. Express its predicted position in the predicted frame k,
and its true position in the true frame k. The gap between the two local positions is d k j.

(Point at the formula.)

FAPE is the mean of d k j over all frames k and all atoms j. Each term is clamped at 10 angstrom,
and the mean is divided by 10 angstrom to make it unitless.

(Point at the inset.)

Two properties. If we rotate or move the whole prediction, every local coordinate stays the same, so
the loss stays the same. Now reflect the prediction. The frames are built from the three backbone
atoms. The first two axes follow the atoms, and the third axis is their cross product, so every
frame is right handed. Reflect all atoms, and the first two axes flip with them, but their cross
product does not. In local coordinates this flips exactly the z coordinate. A mirror image therefore
gets a large loss, while a loss on distances alone gives the same value for both. This is how the
loss tells a protein from its mirror image. The derivation is on backup slide two.


[SLIDE 14. Training: self-distillation, masked MSA, total loss. Ben. Ends at 18:00.]

BEN

How was it trained.

(Point at the left diagram.)

Supervised training on the Protein Data Bank alone already works. Two additions improve it. First,
self distillation. A network trained on the PDB predicts structures for about 350,000 sequences from
a sequence database, Uniclust30, that have no measured structure. Residues with low confidence are
masked out. A new network is trained from scratch, and three in four training examples come from
these predicted structures, one in four from the PDB.

(Point at the right diagram.)

Second, a masked MSA loss. 15 percent of the positions in the alignment are hidden, and the network
must predict the hidden amino acids from its final MSA representation. This follows the masked
language model objective of BERT.

(Point at the loss line.)

The total loss is a weighted sum of five terms, as on the slide. FAPE on the final structure, FAPE
after each of the 8 layers on the C alpha atoms with a torsion angle loss, binned residue distances
from the pair array, the masked MSA loss with the largest weight, two, and the confidence loss on
pLDDT.

So training uses sequences that have no structure.


[SLIDE 15. Ablations: accuracy lost without each component. Ben. Ends at 19:47.]

BEN

Does each constraint matter. An ablation removes one component, retrains the network with three or
four random seeds, and measures the loss in accuracy against the baseline.

(Point at the two axes.)

In the left figure, the first column is GDT on the CASP14 domains. GDT is the global distance test,
the share of C alpha atoms that lie within a few distance cutoffs after the best overlay, from 0 to
100. The second column is lDDT C alpha on recent PDB chains. Zero is the baseline, and bars to the
left are losses.

(Point at the rows with the red numbers.)

One. Give the network only statistics of the alignment, how often each amino acid occurs in each
column and in each pair of columns, instead of the raw sequences. About two GDT points are lost.

Two. Replace the triangle updates, the pair bias and the gates by ordinary attention along the rows
and columns of the pair array. Almost five points are lost.

Three. Replace IPA by a direct linear projection of the frames from the single representation.
Between two and three points are lost. The paper also removed IPA and recycling together, and then
sixteen points are lost. The supplement says that this double ablation must be interpreted with
caution.

(Point at the small plot on the right, Supplementary Figure 9.)

Four. Train with a loss on distances only, instead of FAPE. The GDT splits into two groups. Score
the low group against the mirror image of the truth, and it is high. The supplement concludes in
section 1.9.3 that without FAPE the model often predicts the mirror image.


[SLIDE 16. Interpretation: intermediate structures in the Evoformer. Ben. Ends at 20:38.]

BEN

What happens inside the Evoformer. The authors froze the trained network and trained a separate

(Point at the curves.)

The curves show the GDT of these intermediate structures. LmrP, a multidrug transporter with two

[SLIDE 18. After AlphaFold 2: 2021 to 2025. Ben. Ends at 22:23.]

BEN

(Point down the timeline.)

What followed. On the same day in July 2021, Baek and colleagues published RoseTTAFold, a network
with three parallel tracks for the sequence, the pairs and the coordinates. In the same issue of
Nature, DeepMind predicted the structures of 98.5 percent of the human proteins. AlphaFold-Multimer
extended the network to complexes of several chains. In 2022 ESMFold replaced the alignment by a
protein language model and predicts from one sequence alone, an order of magnitude faster. In 2024
AlphaFold 3 extended prediction to DNA, RNA and ligands, the small molecules that bind to proteins.
In the same year Baker, Hassabis and Jumper received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In 2025
AlphaGenome, from Google DeepMind, predicted gene regulation from DNA sequence.

Both limits from the last slide were addressed directly. Multimer and AlphaFold 3 handle complexes,
and ESMFold removes the alignment.


[SLIDE 19. AlphaFold 3: the four constraints revisited. Ben. Ends at 24:17.]

BEN

AlphaFold 3 is a test of our four constraints. This is its architecture, Figure 1d of the AlphaFold
3 paper.

(Point at the red frame.)

One, the alignment. The MSA module shrinks to four blocks. Their job is to write the alignment into
the pair representation, through the same outer product mean as in AlphaFold 2. After that the
alignment itself is dropped, and only the pair representation goes on. So constraint one survives in
a reduced form, and the paper reports that accuracy still falls for shallow alignments.

(Point at the blue frame.)

Two, the triangles. The Pairformer keeps 48 blocks of triangle updates on the pair representation,
largely unchanged.

(Point at the green frame.)

Three and four, frames, IPA and FAPE. All three are gone. A diffusion module starts from random atom
positions and removes the noise step by step. The symmetry is taught by random rotations and
translations of the training data, and the loss is computed after aligning prediction and truth.
AlphaFold 3 draws several candidate structures and ranks them. The reported costs are overlapping
atoms and, in one benchmark, 4.4 percent of predictions with the wrong hand, although the model
receives the correct chirality as input. Both are penalised in the ranking, which reduces them but
does not remove them.

The paper reports no ablation of the removed parts. Our reading follows an essay by Richard Sutton,
the bitter lesson. It says that methods that learn from data and compute beat methods that build in
human knowledge. Here two of the four constraints were dropped and learned from data instead, at a
measurable cost. The triangles stayed.


[SLIDE 20. Summary: the four constraints and their ablation. Ben. Ends at 24:59.]

BEN

To summarise. Four constraints of the problem, each built into one part of the network.

(Point at the table.)

Attention over the alignment costs about two GDT points when it is removed. The triangles cost
almost five. Replacing IPA, and with it the iteration of the structure module, costs between two and
three. Without FAPE the model builds mirror images.

The paper's own conclusion is that these built in constraints let the network learn from the limited
data in the Protein Data Bank. AlphaFold 3, with far more data, kept one of the four.

EVITA

Question one. pLDDT is trained on the model's own errors. When would you distrust it?

(Answers to collect: proteins with no related sequences, designed proteins, disordered regions, chains in complexes. Then add ours.)

Our addition. pLDDT is itself a prediction, and it was trained only on structures with a resolution
better than 3 angstrom, not on NMR structures. For a protein unlike anything in the Protein Data
Bank, both the structure and the confidence are extrapolations. Low pLDDT is still informative. The
paper on the human proteome that Ben mentioned uses it to find regions that have no fixed structure,
called disordered regions.

BEN

Question two. Accuracy falls below about 30 related sequences. Did the network learn folding, or
evolution?

(Answers to collect: both, evolution first, the MSA is a shortcut. Then add ours.)

Our addition. The paper's own reading is that the alignment is needed to find the coarse fold early
in the network, and that the refinement does not depend on it. ESMFold is evidence on both sides. It
predicts from one sequence, but its language model was trained on tens of millions of sequences, so
the evolution is in its weights.

Question three. AlphaFold 2 built symmetry and chirality in. AlphaFold 3 learns them from data. When
is building in worth it?

(Answers to collect: when data is scarce, when the symmetry is exact, when the cost of a violation is high. Then close with ours.)

Our closing. AlphaFold 2's own supplement makes a surprising observation. Equivariance means that
when the input is rotated, the output rotates with it. The ablation that removes IPA removes every
equivariant component, and the network is still very accurate. AlphaFold 3 had far more data and a
decoder that samples structures, the diffusion module. It dropped the frames and FAPE and pays 4.4
percent wrong chirality, which it patches in the ranking. What it kept is the triangle update on the
pairs. So a built in symmetry pays when data is scarce and the symmetry is exact. The constraint
that lasted is the one on the representation, the pair array with its triangles, not the ones on the
geometry of the output.


[BACKUP 1. IPA: invariance and equivariance. Ben. On request.]

BEN

Three lines. A global motion T global multiplies every frame from the left. In the distance between
a query point of i and a key point of j, T global appears on both sides and cancels, so the
attention weights do not change. The output points are read back in frame i, and T global meets its
inverse, so the output in local coordinates does not change. The frame update is composed in the
frame's own coordinates, so the new frame is T global times the new frame of the unmoved protein.
That is exact equivariance under rotations and translations, the group SE 3, by construction. A
reflection is not covered. The frames are built from the atoms with a right handed rule, so
reflecting the atoms does not act on the frames as one global motion, and the supplement notes that
IPA as well as FAPE can tell a protein from its mirror image. The loss is the main source of the
handedness.


[BACKUP 2. Chirality: where the handedness comes from. Ben. On request.]

BEN

Frames are built from N, C alpha and C by Gram Schmidt, with the third axis the cross product of the
first two, so every frame is right handed. Reflect all atoms. The frames are rebuilt from the
reflected atoms and do not simply reflect with them. In local coordinates exactly the z coordinate
flips. The unclamped FAPE between a structure and its mirror image is twice the sum of the absolute
local z coordinates, a large number. The supplement tested it. A model trained with a loss on
pairwise distances only, called dRMSD, often builds the mirror image, Supplementary Figure 9. The
side chains are a separate matter. They are built from ideal geometry with a fixed handedness, so
even when the backbone comes out mirrored, the side chains do not.
